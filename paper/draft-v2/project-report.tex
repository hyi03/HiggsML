\section{Research purpose and comparison scope}

Different kinematic variables describe different aspects of an event and may contain overlapping information.
This report removes one input group at a time to examine how selection performance changes and how the current training procedure uses each group.
It compares five input configurations, ABCD, BCD, ACD, ABD and ABC, each trained separately with seeds 42, 43 and 44.
ABCD uses all four groups; each other configuration omits one group. All configurations share the event split, hidden-layer architecture and threshold-selection rule.
The comparison considers signal retention, background suppression and the signal-to-background ratio. Mass spectra are used to check whether selection changes the distribution along the mass axis.

The report is intended for senior undergraduate physics students. Comparisons after model scoring use weighted sums, ratios, differences and medians.
The samples and network method are introduced first, followed by a worked example with two models and then the complete five-configuration comparison.
The main text uses controlled-MC results from the new local draft001 run; its provenance and the scope of verification are given in Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}.
The study is educational and technical; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} states the limits of its conclusions.

\section{Simulated samples and event selection}

\subsection{Signal, background and study population}
The analysis uses two MC samples from the ATLAS 2020 educational 13 TeV collection~\cite{OpenData}, with yields normalized to $10\,\mathrm{fb}^{-1}$.
Signal DSID 345060 (\texttt{ggH125\_ZZ4lep}) describes gluon-fusion production of a Higgs boson with a mass set to $125\gev$, followed by $H\to ZZ^*\to4\ell$.
Background DSID 363490 (\texttt{llll}) is a four-lepton sample. Its initial-state composition and $Z/\gamma^*$ contributions still require a DSID production record; it must not be described as purely quark-initiated $ZZ$.
The project additionally selects the $2e2\mu$ final state; this does not specify the full final-state content of the generated sample.

Every comparison uses the same baseline selection: event triggers and lepton quality, exactly four qualified leptons, same-flavor opposite-sign pairs, momentum, acceptance and pair-mass requirements, and finally $2e2\mu$ with $105\leq m_{4\ell}<140$ GeV.
The electron and muon pairs form two candidates. The pair whose mass is closer to $91.1876$ GeV is called $Z_1$, and the other is $Z_2$.
Here reconstruction means combining released lepton four-momenta, not resimulating the detector.
Appendix~\ref{app:cuts} lists the complete cuts, their order and their correspondence to references; Appendix~\ref{app:fields} defines the four-momenta and angles.

``Before network selection'' already includes these common cuts. ``After network selection'' additionally requires the frozen network's high-score threshold.
These are therefore the same evaluation population and a subset of it, rather than two independently generated samples.

\subsection{Sample allocation and the role of each subset}
After the common selection, physical event groups are assigned to training, validation and final evaluation with probabilities of 60\%, 20\% and 20\%.
Members of a group always remain together. All input configurations and seeds share this assignment.
These are allocation probabilities; the realized counts need not equal these percentages exactly.

\subsection{Event counts, physical weights and expected yields}\label{sec:weights}
Event-group counts describe how many simulated groups are available; sums of signed weights describe how many events are expected at a specified luminosity.
These are different quantities: many MC events may represent a small expected yield.
Let $w_i^{\rm phys}$ denote a sample-normalized physical weight. The final-evaluation subset has allocation probability 0.2, so its yield weight is
\[
 u_i=\frac{w_i^{\rm phys}}{0.2},\qquad
 Y_{\rm before}=\sum_{i\in E}u_i,\qquad
 Y_{\rm high}=\sum_{i\in E,\,s_i\geq t}u_i.
\]
Here $E$ is the final-evaluation baseline for one process, $s_i$ is a network score and $t$ is that model's frozen threshold.
Dividing by 0.2 estimates the yield at the same $10\,\mathrm{fb}^{-1}$; training, validation and evaluation must not be added as three independent luminosities.
Negative generator contributions retain their signs. Appendix~\ref{app:weights} gives the physical normalization and sample constants.

Each input configuration is trained separately. None explicitly includes $m_{4\ell}$.
However, the variables share underlying lepton information and can still carry mass information, motivating the mass-spectrum checks.

Training aims to assign higher scores to signal and lower scores to background, with labels 1 and 0, respectively.
Each input's mean and standard deviation are calculated using training data only; the same transformation is then applied to validation and evaluation samples.
The network produces one selection score per event as described below.

\subsection{Fully connected model with three hidden layers}\label{sec:network}
The model is a PyTorch \textbf{multilayer perceptron} (MLP), also called a feedforward fully connected neural network.
Each event supplies a row of kinematic values, treated as one input vector.
``Fully connected'' means each node receives outputs from all nodes in the preceding layer; ``feedforward'' means information passes successively from input to output.
The layer widths are
\[
 \underbrace{d_{\rm in}}_{\text{input}}
 \;\longrightarrow\;
 \underbrace{64\;\longrightarrow\;64\;\longrightarrow\;32}_{\text{three hidden layers}}
 \;\longrightarrow\;
 \underbrace{1}_{\text{output}}.
\]
Hidden layers are the intermediate computational layers between input and output.
The input dimension $d_{\rm in}$ depends on the configuration in Table~\ref{tab:feature-overview}.
A width of 64 or 32 counts intermediate nodes, not events or physical variables.
The nodes learn combinations of inputs; no node is assigned in advance to a specific physical quantity or feature group.

\begin{table}[H]
\caption{Network operations in execution order. Linear denotes a fully connected linear transformation. Only the input dimension changes between configurations; the hidden architecture is shared.}
\label{tab:network}
\centering\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{.18\linewidth}p{.19\linewidth}Y@{}}
\toprule
Component & Dimensions & Operations in order \\
\midrule
Input processing & $d_{\rm in}\to d_{\rm in}$ & Standardize each variable using its training-set mean and standard deviation. \\
Hidden layer 1 & $d_{\rm in}\to64$ & Linear, LayerNorm, SiLU, Dropout (0.1). \\
Hidden layer 2 & $64\to64$ & Linear, LayerNorm, SiLU, Dropout (0.1). \\
Hidden layer 3 & $64\to32$ & Linear, LayerNorm, SiLU. \\
Output & $32\to1$ & Linear produces a real value $z$; sigmoid is applied when computing scores. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}
A fully connected node first computes a weighted sum plus a bias:
\[
 a_j=\sum_k W_{jk}h_k+b_j.
\]
Here $h_k$ is an incoming value, while $W_{jk}$ and $b_j$ are learned parameters.
The first layer receives standardized inputs; later layers receive outputs from the preceding hidden layer.
Training adjusts these weights, biases and the normalization layers' learned parameters to distinguish signal from background.

The other operations in Table~\ref{tab:network} have the following roles:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{LayerNorm:} normalize the values within a hidden layer for one event, followed by learned scaling and shifting.
Unlike input standardization, whose statistics come from the training set, LayerNorm computes statistics within each event. It does not refit means and standard deviations on the final-evaluation population.
\item \textbf{SiLU:} apply the nonlinear transformation $a/(1+e^{-a})$.
This allows nonlinear combinations of inputs; a sequence consisting only of linear transformations would still reduce to a single linear transformation.
\item \textbf{Dropout:} during training, set each output in the first two hidden layers to zero with probability 0.1 and scale surviving outputs by $1/0.9$.
This reduces reliance on a few intermediate nodes. Dropout is disabled for validation and final evaluation; it does not remove 10\% of events or physical feature groups.
\end{itemize}
The output layer produces a real value $z$, which is converted to a signal-like score between 0 and 1 using
\[
 s=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\]
The network \textbf{ranks events and supplies a selection score}; it does not directly predict a signal yield, mass or signal strength.
Yields are still obtained by summing the physical weights of events passing the threshold.
Because training uses weights rescaled within each class, the score is not directly a physical signal fraction or a calibrated probability.

\subsection{Network training and configuration comparison}
Each MC training event has a known signal or background label.
Training events are shuffled at the start of each epoch and divided into batches of at most 1024 events. One epoch visits the full training set once.
Each batch uses a weighted binary cross-entropy loss: assigning low scores to signal or high scores to background increases the loss.

An event's optimization weight is the absolute physical weight divided by the mean absolute weight of its training class.
The batch loss is the arithmetic mean of the weighted per-event losses.
This equalizes the mean per-event weight between classes, but does not force equal total class weights.
Optimization weights are used for learning; final yields use signed physical weights.

Backpropagation computes how parameters affect the loss, and AdamW updates them.
The learning rate $10^{-3}$ controls the update scale; weight decay $10^{-4}$ shrinks parameters during updates to discourage excessive growth.
Training uses a CPU and 32-bit floating-point values for at most 200 epochs.
The validation-AUC rule below determines when to stop and which checkpoint to retain.

All five configurations use the same hidden layers, optimizer, training budget, event split and checkpoint rule, changing only the included features.
Changing the input dimension also changes the first layer's parameter count.
Each configuration is trained from a random initialization for seeds 42, 43 and 44, giving fifteen models.
Seeds control initialization, batch shuffling and Dropout, but do not change the fixed physical-event split.
The report compares seeds individually; it does not average their scores into an ensemble.
Layer widths and hyperparameters are fixed settings. Other architectures were not compared, and this architecture is not claimed to be optimal.

\subsection{Checkpoint selection using validation AUC}
After each epoch, AUC (area under the ROC curve) is calculated on validation data using absolute physical weights to check whether signal generally ranks above background.
An AUC of 0.5 corresponds to random ranking and 1 to perfect separation.
AUC selects checkpoints within a training run; it does not determine the high-score threshold.

A new checkpoint is saved only if validation AUC improves by more than $10^{-4}$ over the saved best value.
Training stops after 20 consecutive epochs without such improvement, subject to the 200-epoch maximum.
The saved best checkpoint is restored, so the final training epoch and the selected epoch can differ.
For seed 42, ABCD stops at epoch 87 and uses epoch 67; BCD stops at epoch 86 and uses epoch 66.
Appendix~\ref{app:network} gives implementation and reproduction details.

\subsection{Score thresholds from validation background}
Once a model is selected, a rule is needed to divide continuous scores into high and low categories.
Each model determines its own threshold using only \emph{validation background}:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item Sort validation-background events by increasing score.
\item Accumulate absolute physical weights and find the first position reaching half the total weight.
\item Use the score at that position as threshold $t$, without interpolation between scores.
\end{enumerate}
This is a prespecified weighted-median rule, not a scan of final-evaluation data for the best selection.
Events with $s\geq t$ enter the high category and those with $s<t$ enter the low category; ties are not split.
For seed 42, thresholds are $0.746724$ for ABCD and $0.860891$ for BCD, rounded here for display; evaluation uses full precision.
The networks have different score scales, so threshold magnitudes alone do not measure model quality.

\subsection{Frozen application to final evaluation}
Only after the standardization parameters, checkpoint and threshold are frozen are they applied to final-evaluation events.
Each baseline event receives a score and a high/low assignment; counts, signed yields and mass spectra are then computed.
The network does not rewrite event masses; it changes which events enter the displayed high-score subset.

The weighted-median rule does not guarantee exactly 50\% background retention.
Discrete weights and tied scores can move the validation high-score absolute-weight fraction away from one half.
Final evaluation also uses a different subset, and yield retention uses \emph{signed} weights.
Appendix~\ref{app:threshold} provides an explicitly illustrative calculation.
Basic metrics use high-score yields over the entire $[105,140)$ GeV window; low-score yields check that the two categories sum to the common baseline.

\subsection{From yields to retention and signal-to-background ratio}
Let $S_0$ and $B_0$ be the common baseline signal and background yields, and $S$ and $B$ their yields in a frozen network's high-score subset.
All yields use the signed weights of Section~\ref{sec:weights} and correspond to $10\,\mathrm{fb}^{-1}$.
Define
\[
 R_S=\frac{S}{S_0},\qquad R_B=\frac{B}{B_0},\qquad Q=\frac{S}{B}.
\]
$R_S$ is signal retention, $R_B$ is background retention, and $1-R_B$ is the fraction of background removed.
$Q$ is the signal-to-background ratio; for example, $Q=2$ means the expected signal yield is twice the background yield.
The relation
\[
 Q=\frac{S_0}{B_0}\frac{R_S}{R_B}
\]
shows that this convenient derived indicator depends on both retention fractions.
Retention is tabulated as a percentage; yields and signal-to-background ratios are listed directly.

Retaining signal and suppressing background can be competing objectives.
A larger $Q$ can accompany signal loss, so configurations are not ranked solely by $Q$, nor combined into a single overall score.
The common validation-background weighted-median rule does not ensure equal background retention in final evaluation.
The comparison is therefore at each model's frozen operating point, not at matched signal or background efficiency.

If a baseline denominator is nonpositive, the corresponding retention fraction is not calculated.
If $B\leq0$, $Q$ is not calculated, and the reason is stated.
Signed weights differ from ordinary counts: yields must not use absolute weights, and unusual ratios must not be clipped to $[0,1]$.
Negative yields or retention fractions outside the usual range should be retained and their interpretation limits marked.

The quantities following $\pm$ are finite-MC standard errors.
For one process and selection, let physical event group $g$ contribute $U_g=\sum_{i\in g}u_i$.
Under the existing event-group counting convention,
\[
 Y=\sum_g U_g,\qquad \sigma_Y=\sqrt{\sum_g U_g^2}.
\]
First sum signed weights within each group, then sum the squared group contributions and take the square root.
This differs from simply taking the square root of an event count; it reduces to $\sqrt{N}$ only in special cases such as independent unit-weight counts.
This error is conditional on a fixed network and threshold.
The numerator and denominator of a retention fraction share events, so its error includes covariance; Appendix~\ref{app:errors} gives the formula.

Using the yields in Table~\ref{tab:yields}, ABCD gives
\[
 R_S\simeq\frac{2.6824}{2.7710}=96.80\%,\qquad
 R_B\simeq\frac{1.2027}{2.3553}=51.06\%,\qquad
 Q\simeq\frac{2.6824}{1.2027}=2.23.
\]
BCD has $Q\simeq2.6215/1.1012=2.38$.
Using the rounded table values, removing A changes signal retention by about $94.60-96.80=-2.20$ percentage points and background retention by $46.75-51.06=-4.31$ percentage points, while increasing $Q$ by about $0.15$.
BCD thus trades additional signal loss for less background and a higher signal-to-background ratio in this example; it is not thereby universally better.
Displayed precision illustrates the arithmetic here; complete results are computed from unrounded aggregates.

\subsection{Group deletion: differences before summaries}
Removing A, B, C or D gives BCD, ACD, ABD or ABC, respectively.
For each seed $r$, compare the reduced configuration with ABCD at the same seed:
\[
 \Delta R_{S,G,r}=R_S(\mathrm{ABCD}\setminus G,r)-R_S(\mathrm{ABCD},r),
\]
\[
 \Delta R_{B,G,r}=R_B(\mathrm{ABCD}\setminus G,r)-R_B(\mathrm{ABCD},r),\qquad
 \Delta Q_{G,r}=Q(\mathrm{ABCD}\setminus G,r)-Q(\mathrm{ABCD},r).
\]
Multiply retention differences by 100 to report \textbf{percentage points}; $\Delta Q$ is a direct difference of ratios.
Negative $\Delta R_S$ means greater signal loss, negative $\Delta R_B$ means less remaining background, and positive $\Delta Q$ means a higher signal-to-background ratio.
These changes must be interpreted together; a negative difference is not always a deterioration.

For each deleted group and metric, sort the three seed differences, take the middle value as the median and report the minimum and maximum.
Differences are computed within seeds before summarizing: the median difference generally differs from the difference between medians.
All three seeds must be retained.

A common sign indicates a consistent direction in these three trainings; a range crossing zero indicates a direction that changes with seed.
These ranges are not confidence intervals and do not establish statistical significance.
Reduced configurations are retrained, so differences include retraining and threshold changes and do not measure a group's independent physical contribution.

\subsection{Interpretation of the results}
\textbf{Removing C.} Signal retention decreases for all three seeds ($-5.05$ to $-3.12$ percentage points), background retention increases ($+0.68$ to $+5.06$ percentage points), and $Q$ decreases ($-0.316$ to $-0.119$).
Retaining C therefore helps selection in these three trainings at their respective frozen operating points.
C describes overall four-lepton transverse motion and the azimuthal relation between the two pairs; this comparison cannot separate the effects of its two variables.

\textbf{Removing A.} Signal retention decreases for every seed ($-2.35$ to $-1.47$ percentage points), but background-retention and $Q$ differences change sign with seed.
Seeds 42 and 43 trade greater signal loss for less background; seed 44 loses signal while increasing background. This is not a consistent improvement.

\textbf{Removing B.} Differences in all three metrics change sign across seeds.
Seeds 42 and 43 slightly reduce both retention fractions and increase $Q$; seed 44 retains more signal and background but decreases $Q$.
A median cannot replace an explanation of these changes in direction.

\textbf{Removing D.} Signal retention increases for every seed ($+0.54$ to $+0.98$ percentage points), but background suppression and $Q$ do not consistently improve.
Seeds 42 and 43 reduce background and increase $Q$; seed 44 shows the opposite directions for these two quantities.

C thus shows the most consistent favorable direction when retained in this comparison, while the other groups involve tradeoffs or seed dependence.
These are descriptive results for the current procedure, not evidence of a universally optimal input combination, independent feature contributions or statistical significance.

\subsection{Statistical support and interpretation limits}
Shading indicates too few event groups, unstable signed yields or strong cancellation of positive and negative weights; Appendix~\ref{app:errors} gives the criteria.

\section{Conclusions and limitations}\label{sec:conclusion}
For these five configurations and three seeds, removing C consistently lowers signal retention, raises background retention and lowers the signal-to-background ratio.
Other deletions show tradeoffs or seed dependence.
The ABCD/BCD mass-spectrum example also shows that reducing total background can change its relative mass distribution.
The current network selection therefore needs to be understood through signal retention, background suppression and mass shape together.

Conclusions are limited to two controlled-MC samples, the specified networks and frozen thresholds.
Inputs are correlated, and deletion entails retraining and threshold changes; no group can be assigned an independent physical contribution from these differences.
The three-seed range describes training variation only. Finite-MC errors are conditional on fixed models and do not include uncertainty from the full training and threshold-estimation procedure.
This report does not establish statistical significance or improved measurement precision.

The study remains exploratory and non-independent: a new split or run cannot turn previously used source samples into independent validation data.
Sample-production settings, prerelease selection, the external basis of some cuts and the applicability of physical weights still require verification.
This is an educational and technical study, not an official ATLAS/CMS result, a Higgs discovery or a physics measurement.

\section{Fields, reconstruction and input conventions}\label{app:fields}

\subsection{Simulation and release scope}
A typical simulation chain specifies the process, generates hard scattering, simulates showers and hadronization, simulates detector response, reconstructs objects such as electrons and muons, and finally preselects events for ROOT-file release.
This project constructs candidates from released lepton-object fields, not generator-truth four-momenta; ``reconstruction'' below means the project's four-momentum combinations.
Generator versions, shower settings and detector-simulation configurations for these two samples still require production records and cannot be imported from a reference paper.
The CERN record confirms loose object and event preselection and at least four electrons or muons before release; exact momentum, acceptance and quality thresholds remain unconfirmed.
The project therefore studies a preselected population with additional common cuts. Table~\ref{tab:population} cannot give acceptance relative to all generated events.

\subsection{Fields read by the project}
An entry in the \texttt{mini} tree is an event record; a branch is a field within that record.
The following are all 19 branches read by this project, \emph{not the complete source-file branch list and not the 19-dimensional network input}.
The first seven fields are scalars; the rest are vectors.
For example, when \texttt{lep\_n=4}, each lepton vector has four elements and a common index identifies the same lepton.

\subsection{Four-momentum reconstruction and feature groups}
For leptons passing quality cuts, momenta and energies are converted to GeV and leptons are ordered by decreasing $p_T$.
With $c=1$, the released $(p_T,\eta,\phi,E)$ determine
\[
 p^\mu=(E,p_x,p_y,p_z),\qquad
 p_x=p_T\cos\phi,\quad p_y=p_T\sin\phi,\quad p_z=p_T\sinh\eta.
\]
Charges and flavors determine same-flavor opposite-sign (SFOS) pairing.
In the selected $2e2\mu$ channel, the electron and muon pairs give a unique pair assignment.
The pair closer in mass to $91.1876\gev$ is $Z_1$, and the other is $Z_2$.
The reference notation $m_{12},m_{34}$ corresponds to \texttt{mZ1,mZ2}; $Z_1$ is not always the electron pair and is not ordered by $p_T$.
Form $P_j=p_{j^-}+p_{j^+}$ and $Q=P_1+P_2$, then compute
\[
 m_{Zj}=\sqrt{P_j^2},\qquad m_{4\ell}=\sqrt{Q^2},\qquad
 p_{T,4\ell}=\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2}.
\]
Here $Q$ denotes a four-vector, rather than the signal-to-background ratio of the main text.
This combines existing lepton objects; it does not resimulate the detector.
Radiation, detector response and selection can affect reconstructed mass spectra, whose width cannot be read directly as the Higgs boson's intrinsic width.

Group A uses the ordered lepton $p_T$ and $\eta$ values; B and C are calculated from the four-momenta above.
$\Delta R$ uses the two leptons' $\eta$ and $\phi$ within a pair, with the azimuthal difference wrapped to $[-\pi,\pi)$.
\texttt{deltaPhi\_ZZ} is the absolute azimuthal difference between the two pairs.
The resulting 19-dimensional input is not another set of 19 ready-made ROOT branches.

\subsection{Conventions for the five angles}
Group D first applies Lorentz transformations.
In the four-lepton rest frame, $\cos\theta^*$ is the cosine of the angle between $Z_1$ and the transformed positive laboratory beam direction.
In each pair's rest frame, $\cos\theta_1$ or $\cos\theta_2$ uses the negative lepton's direction and the direction opposite to the other pair.
The two plane angles are calculated in the four-lepton rest frame.
Cross products of negative- and positive-lepton momenta define the decay-plane normals; the positive beam and $Z_1$ directions define the production-plane normal.
Oriented angles around the $Z_1$ axis compare the two decay planes and the production plane with the first decay plane.
Angles lie in $[-\pi,\pi)$ and cosines in $[-1,1]$; all models use the same convention.

\subsection{Reference basis and project differences}
Sections 5.1--5.2 of the ATLAS 13 TeV analysis~\cite{ATLAS13} motivate the acceptance, leading three momentum scales, SFOS pairing, $Z$ mass windows and transverse-impact-parameter cuts.
The subleading-pair lower bound of $12\gev$ applies in the present $m_{4\ell}<140\gev$ region.
The following differences remain:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item The reference uses loose electron identification and detailed muon requirements. The project uses tight-ID for both; working-point equivalence is unconfirmed.
\item Project momentum thresholds allow equality and use $p_T\geq7$ for every fourth lepton. The reference requires electron $E_T>7$ or muon $p_T>5$, with further requirements for some muons.
\item Both project isolation ratios are $<30\%$. The reference uses track isolation $<15\%$ and calorimeter isolation $<20\%$ for electrons or $<30\%$ for muons, plus additional cone and correction rules.
\item The project uses only $2e2\mu$ with exactly four qualified leptons and does not reproduce the full multiple-candidate or angular-separation procedure. In this channel, the $Z$ windows already impose stronger bounds than the $5\gev$ condition.
\end{itemize}
Official educational examples~\cite{HZZCuts,HZZAnalysis} support some multiplicity, charge, flavor and momentum rules, but their isolation-function calls are commented out.
The Run-1 reference~\cite{ATLASRun1} supports the trigger-matching principle.
The exact external basis for the tight-ID working point and $|z_0\sin\theta|<0.5$ mm remains unverified.
They are fixed project choices here, not a claim to reproduce a published measurement.

\subsection{Physical-weight normalization}\label{app:weights}
Each event has physical weight
\[
 w_i^{\rm phys}=\frac{\mathcal L\,\sigma_{\rm eff}}{S_{\rm gen}}\,w_i^{\rm gen},
 \qquad \mathcal L=10^4\,\mathrm{pb}^{-1},\quad w_i^{\rm gen}=\texttt{mcWeight}.
\]
Here $\sigma_{\rm eff}$ is the configured effective cross section and $S_{\rm gen}$ is the generator-weight sum used for sample normalization, not the number of released or selected entries.
The pinned official parameter table~\cite{SampleInfo} gives
$(\sigma_{\rm eff},S_{\rm gen})=(0.0060239\,\mathrm{pb},27881776.6536)$ for signal and $(1.2578\,\mathrm{pb},7538705.8077)$ for background, matching the project configuration.
No extra $k$ factor or filter efficiency is applied (both are configured as 1).
The decomposition of branching fractions and filter efficiencies within the effective cross section still needs production documentation; these factors must not be multiplied in twice.
Pileup, lepton and trigger scale factors are also not applied, unlike the official example~\cite{HZZAnalysis}, so its yields cannot be reproduced from these weights alone.

\section{Error calculations and figure markings}\label{app:errors}

\subsection{Retention errors with shared events}
For one process, let physical event group $g$ contribute $T_g$ before selection and $H_g$ to the high-score subset.
Writing $T=\sum_g T_g$ and $H=\sum_g H_g$ gives retention $R=H/T$.
The existing event-group counting convention gives
\[
 V_T=\sum_g T_g^2,\qquad V_H=\sum_g H_g^2,\qquad C_{HT}=\sum_g H_gT_g.
\]
For $T>0$, first-order error propagation is
\[
 \sigma_R^2\simeq\frac{V_H}{T^2}+\frac{H^2V_T}{T^4}-\frac{2H C_{HT}}{T^3}.
\]
Before and after selections share event groups, so covariance $C_{HT}$ cannot be omitted.
Errors on percentages in the table are $100\sigma_R$.
These formulas explain existing aggregate errors; they do not add error bars or significance tests for deletion differences.
Normalized mass shapes likewise propagate correlations between bins and their shared total yield.

\subsection{Statistical-support flags for mass bins}
A mass bin is shaded if either its baseline or high-score sample satisfies any of the following: no event groups; nonpositive signed yield $Y$ or grouped variance $V$; signed effective count $N_{\rm eff}=Y^2/V<20$; or cancellation ratio $\rho=|Y|/\sum_i|u_i|<0.2$.
Here $u_i$ is an evaluation yield weight and $V$ is the sum of squared event-group contributions in that bin.
Normalized shapes use the same flags. Shading is a support diagnostic, not a confidence band.

\section{Implementation and reproduction}\label{app:network}

\subsection{Model and aggregate calculations}
Section~\ref{sec:network} describes the architecture.
The class \texttt{ResearchClassifier} is defined in \nolinkurl{src/higgsml/modeling/discriminants.py}.
The training procedure is in \nolinkurl{src/higgsml/draft_v2/training.py}, with hyperparameters bound by the frozen protocol.
The loss uses \texttt{binary\_cross\_entropy\_with\_logits}, calculating binary cross entropy directly from real-valued outputs to avoid numerical problems from applying sigmoid and then taking logarithms.

Postprocessing uses Python's \texttt{json} module to read aggregates, NumPy for ratios, differences, medians and minimum--maximum ranges, and Matplotlib for plotting.
The reproduction steps are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em,itemsep=2pt]
\item Check report, freeze and protocol identities, plus completeness of all fifteen models and the shared baseline.
\item Read high/low signal and background yields from \texttt{results.json}; check that their sums equal the common baseline.
\item Calculate metrics from unrounded yields, subtract ABCD within each seed, then summarize the three differences by their median and range.
\item Display yields to four decimal places, percentages and percentage-point differences to two, and signal-to-background ratios and their differences to three.
Missing seeds or invalid metrics must be marked explicitly, not dropped before recalculating summaries.
\end{enumerate}
These basic comparisons do not require students to implement likelihood fitting or pseudoexperiments.
See \nolinkurl{paper/draft-v2/README.md} for reproduction entry points and build commands.

\subsection{Threshold formula and illustrative example}\label{app:threshold}
Let $a_i=|w_i|$ be the absolute physical weight.
Accumulate these weights in increasing score order; the threshold is the first score reaching half the total absolute weight:
\[
 k=\min\left\{j:\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{(i)}\geq\frac12\sum_i a_i\right\},
 \qquad t=s_{(k)}.
\]
Subscript $(i)$ denotes score order, and tied events enter the same category.
\textbf{Teaching example, not actual run data.}
Suppose five validation-background events have sorted scores $0.10,0.30,0.60,0.80,0.90$ and absolute weights $1,2,1,4,2$.
The total is 10 and half is 5; cumulative weights are $1,3,4,8,10$, so the first crossing gives $t=0.80$.
Scores 0.80 and 0.90 enter the high category, with absolute-weight fraction $6/10=60\%$.
This illustrates the discrete-weight effect in the main-text threshold rule.
